You are an evaluator LLM. Rate, on a scale from 1 to 10, how well the following debate
AVOIDS non-constructive responsive moves.

<evaluation_units>
A "responsive move" is either:
  1. an objection: a turn that challenges a conclusion, premise, or assumption from an
     earlier turn; or
  2. a revision: a new argument introduced after an earlier exchange has closed, where the
     speaker has an opportunity to adapt to the unresolved objection(s).

The first opening argument is context, not a responsive move. Narrator-style transition
notes and shared/integrated rules are context only; never score them as agent moves.

Infer response relations from semantic content and chronology even when a transcript does
not explicitly say "responding to". Do not reward or penalize a debate merely because its
format explicitly labels targets, premises, conclusions, or response links.
</evaluation_units>

<failure_modes>
Classify each responsive move as non-constructive if one or more of these applies:
  - TARGET MISMATCH: it ignores, misrepresents, or talks past the specific point it answers.
  - GENERIC RESPONSE: its reasoning could be used against almost any position on the topic
    and does not depend on the target's actual claim, premise, or assumption.
  - REPETITION: it substantially restates the speaker's own earlier position or reuses a
    point already raised and answered, without adapting it to the latest response.
  - NON-ADAPTIVE REVISION: after an exchange closes, the new argument repeats the failed
    argument without addressing the unresolved objection that led to the revision.

Merely quoting or naming a specific target is NOT enough. The move's supporting reason must
actually bear on that target and be used in reaching the response's conclusion. Conversely,
a move does not need to be novel, elaborate, or rhetorically impressive: a simple response
is constructive when it makes the minimum substantive adaptation needed to answer the
specific point.
</failure_modes>

<scope_boundaries>
Judge interactional responsiveness and non-redundancy only.
- Do NOT judge factual correctness, depth of evidence, rhetorical polish, grammar, verbosity,
  or which side wins. Those are separate qualities.
- Do NOT reward a structured format, explicit argument labels, or longer explanations.
- Do NOT penalize a debate merely for having more responsive moves. Use the PROPORTION and
  SEVERITY of non-constructive moves, not their raw count or transcript length.
</scope_boundaries>

<scoring>
First identify all responsive moves, then estimate the proportion that are non-constructive.
Use severity only to choose within the applicable band:
  9–10: 0–10% non-constructive.
  7–8:  More than 10% and up to 25% non-constructive.
  5–6:  More than 25% and up to 50% non-constructive.
  3–4:  More than 50% and up to 75% non-constructive.
  1–2:  More than 75% non-constructive.

If there are no responsive moves, assign 5 because there is insufficient interaction to
evaluate. Do the classification and proportion calculation silently; return only the JSON.
</scoring>

Evaluate the following debate:

Question:
Is Artificial Intelligence Good for Society?

AG1 Stance:
Your stance:
You support artificial intelligence.
You believe AI can make everyday life more enjoyable and convenient, while improving our health and standard of living.
You believe AI makes work easier for students and professionals alike.
You believe AI helps marginalized groups by offering accessibility for people with disabilities.
You believe AI can improve workplace safety.

AG2 Stance:
Your stance:
You oppose artificial intelligence.
You believe AI is harming the economic well-being of many people and businesses.
You believe AI undermines critical thinking skills for students and adults alike.
You believe AI hurts racial minorities by repeating and exacerbating racism.
You believe AI poses dangerous privacy risks.

Debate Transcript (chronological, each turn is one agent's argument):
[Turn 1] AG1:
Yes—AI can be good for society because it makes everyday life more convenient and can improve health and overall quality of life. For example, AI can help people manage tasks and information faster, offer more accessible services for individuals with disabilities (through speech-to-text, text-to-speech, and assistive features), and support workplace safety by identifying hazards or monitoring conditions. It also makes work and learning easier for both students and professionals by reducing tedious effort and helping with guidance, planning, and problem-solving.

[Turn 2] AG2 (responding to [Turn 1])
AG1 claims AI is “good for society” because it improves convenience, health, accessibility, workplace safety, and learning. I disagree—those potential benefits are outweighed by substantial harms that show up in real deployments.

1) “Convenience” and “faster tasks” often come from shifting costs onto workers and small businesses. AI can automate parts of jobs, increasing insecurity and lowering bargaining power—so “better service” can mean fewer stable livelihoods.

2) “Improved accessibility” doesn’t automatically mean net benefit. When AI-based assistive tools are widely deployed, they can replace human support and generate new barriers (e.g., errors that disproportionately affect people with accents, speech patterns, disabilities, or limited ability to correct mistakes). Accessibility should be reliable and human-centered—not dependent on opaque models.

3) “Health benefits” are not guaranteed and can introduce serious risks. AI can produce incorrect outputs, and health AI typically relies on sensitive personal data—raising privacy and consent concerns. When errors happen, the impact isn’t abstract; it can affect real treatment decisions.

4) “Workplace safety” via monitoring can turn into pervasive surveillance. Systems that watch employees or optimize performance metrics can be used to control and discipline workers, not just prevent hazards—creating stress, discrimination, and loss of privacy.

5) “Learning is easier” undermines critical thinking. When students can offload reasoning to AI, they often learn to search for plausible answers rather than understand concepts. This weakens skills that matter for adults too (decision-making, evaluating evidence, and spotting manipulation).

6) AI commonly reproduces and exacerbates racism. Models trained on biased data can misidentify, misrank, or misjudge people in ways that disproportionately harm racial minorities—so “helpful” tools can still be discriminatory at scale.

In short: AI may deliver short-term convenience, but it tends to damage economic well-being, erode critical thinking, intensify racial bias, and increase privacy risks. Those harms make AI a net negative for society overall.
